\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}

\emph{RQ1---Observed applicability classifications.}
Within the sampled requirements and catalog, the LLM predominantly
returned insufficient information: \StudyInsufficientInformationCount{}
of \StudyAssessmentCount{} pairs
(\RqOneInsufficientInformationPercent{}\%).
It identified \StudyApplicableCount{} pairs as applicable
(\RqOneApplicablePercent{}\%), spanning \StudyPositiveStoryCount{}
of \StudyStoryCount{} stories; \StudyNotApplicableCount{} pairs were
classified as not applicable (Table~\ref{tab:applicability-results}).
Thus, the output is a bounded set of story-specific pattern candidates,
not a universal recommendation list. The dominant unresolved category
limits what can be concluded about catalog applicability from this run:
it does not establish that those patterns are unsuitable or that the
requirements are defective. Likewise, positive classifications are model
judgments, not validated architectural choices. Differences across stories
describe this sample; explaining them would require evidence beyond the
reported label frequencies.

\emph{RQ2---Overlap with the frozen retrieval baseline.}
The methods' outputs intersect on \StudyRagOverlapCount{} of the
\StudyApplicableCount{} LLM-positive pairs
(\RqTwoOverlapPercent{}\%), across \StudyRagOverlapStoryCount{} stories;
\StudyRagOutsideCount{} positive pairs lie outside the same-story final
Top-5 (Table~\ref{tab:rag-comparison}).
This answers a candidate-membership question: the final Top-5 does not
contain most pairs labeled applicable by the exhaustive assessment.
It does not establish which method makes better architectural selections.
The selected-pair set is not an independent reference, and the two
protocols differ in input language and catalog representation.
Consequently, non-overlap cannot be interpreted as retrieval error,
additional validated discoveries, or superiority of exhaustive assessment.

The expert judgments remain unknown. The complete, traceable ADR outputs

\subsection{Interpreting Exhaustive Assessment and Retrieval}
\label{sec:llm-rag-discussion}

The two protocols have different decision boundaries.
Exhaustive assessment evaluates each story--pattern pair against the
supplied requirements and returns an applicability category with
supporting evidence for positive classifications
(Section~\ref{sec:applicability-experiment}).
The retrieval baseline instead ranks catalog documents by their
relationship to a story query and retains a fixed-size candidate set
(Section~\ref{sec:rag-baseline}). A ranking position is not an
applicability judgment: even a story with no LLM-positive pair receives
retrieval candidates. Conversely, exhaustive assessment is not constrained
to return a fixed number of positives. Their intersection therefore
describes agreement in candidate membership, not agreement on a common
classification task.

Table~\ref{tab:rag-comparison} shows that the frozen final Top-5 omits
most LLM-positive pairs. This observation neither proves that retrieval
misses appropriate patterns nor validates the additional LLM selections.
English retrieval queries, Portuguese assessment inputs and unequal
catalog representations prevent attributing the difference solely to
the selection mechanism. The current comparison also does not establish
why individual candidates rank outside the cutoff. Architectural quality
and relevance require evidence independent of either output set; expert
assessment of selected pairs alone cannot establish recall across the
unassessed alternatives.

\emph{Future design hypothesis---not evaluated.}
Retrieval could serve as a candidate-generation stage before pairwise
LLM assessment, potentially reducing the number of pairs sent for
assessment. Such filtering would also exclude the current LLM-positive
pairs outside the cutoff from consideration; the architectural importance
of those exclusions is unknown. No hybrid pipeline was executed, and
end-to-end cost savings, latency and quality effects are
\textbf{not evaluated}, not demonstrated benefits.
A controlled follow-up should align input language and catalog fields,
vary the candidate budget, account for retrieval/reranking overhead
alongside generation costs, and independently assess candidates both
inside and outside the shortlist. Until those trade-offs are measured,
retrieval-first assessment remains a proposed alternative rather than
a validated replacement for exhaustive assessment.

\subsection{Practical Implications for Architects (Partial)}
\label{sec:practitioner-implications}

The current evidence supports treating ADR4AI outputs as material for
decision review, not autonomous architectural authority.
Requirement references and explicit pattern rationales identify claims
an architect can inspect; source/prompt provenance and structured ADR
drafts preserve what was generated for each selected pair
(Sections~\ref{sec:applicability-experiment} and~\ref{sec:adr-results}).
These features make the reasoning available for scrutiny, but do not
establish that references entail the recommendation, that consequences
are complete, or that drafts improve practice. Their value as review aids
remains a hypothesis pending professional assessment.

\emph{Proposed review checkpoints---not a validated workflow.}
Before generation, confirm permission to process the requirements and
whether the supplied story and acceptance criteria capture the necessary
architectural context. For each recommendation, check the cited evidence
against the requirements, examine the pattern's fit and alternatives,
and identify unsupported assumptions or missing constraints.
Before accepting an ADR, review its decision and consequences against
system-specific quality requirements and implementation constraints;
record revisions and explicit architect approval separately from
generation completion. External processing and release require the
governance confirmations in Section~\ref{sec:ethics-reproducibility};
the artifacts do not establish that these safeguards are operational.

Unsafe uses include treating the application-assigned \texttt{Proposed}
status as acceptance, nonempty/schema-valid fields as semantic correctness,
or an insufficient-information label as proof that a pattern is unsuitable.
Neither a retrieved rank nor an LLM-positive classification warrants
automatic deployment. The available evidence does not justify replacing
architect review, claiming production readiness, or promising reduced
decision effort.

\subsection{Research Implications and Future Work}
\label{sec:research-implications}

Requirement-grounded, pattern-by-pattern assessment makes the unit of
recommendation explicit and preserves an inspectable link between each
positive classification and its cited requirement evidence.
Exhaustive coverage also retains non-positive outcomes rather than
reporting recommendations alone (Table~\ref{tab:applicability-results}).
This provides an auditable basis for studying selection behavior, not
proof of semantic grounding. The predominance of insufficient-information
labels motivates testing how architectural context affects classification,
but does not identify missing context as the cause of those labels.
Catalog scope, prompt rules and model behavior are alternative factors
that the current run does not disentangle.

The study connects selection to documentation through a one-to-one
story--pattern-to-ADR mapping (Section~\ref{sec:adr-results}).
This connection permits examining the proposed decision together with
the selection rationale and requirements. However, the stages are
dependent: an unsupported selection can lead to a complete but
inappropriate ADR. Reproducible compilation validates artifact lineage,
not decision quality, and the generated ADR is not independent
confirmation of its underlying classification.

\emph{Proposed follow-up experiments---not evaluated.}
Replication with additional systems and catalogs should vary domain,
requirement detail and pattern coverage explicitly rather than imply
generalization from this sample. Controlled comparisons of models and
prompt variants should keep requirement inputs and catalog versions
fixed, retain all classifications and record repeated-run variability.
A context-enrichment experiment could compare the same cases before
and after adding verified architectural constraints, distinguishing
changes in output frequencies from independently assessed improvements.

Independent expert assessment of sampled non-positive pairs, or an
all-pattern reference for a bounded subset, could examine applicability
beyond the selected cases and support classification-error measures
when the reference judgments are sufficiently established. The planned
selected-pair questionnaire alone cannot do so. Selection and ADR quality
should also be assessed separately to distinguish recommendation
limitations from documentation limitations.
Cost and latency evaluation should measure end-to-end execution,
including failed attempts, retrieval/reranking and human review where
applicable, rather than infer savings from completed-output counts.
The shortlist experiment proposed in
Section~\ref{sec:llm-rag-discussion} would then test resource--quality
trade-offs. None of these extensions has been executed or establishes
an evaluated contribution of the present study.
